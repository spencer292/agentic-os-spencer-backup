# Backing Yourself — draft manuscript (chapters 1–3)

## Chapter 1 — The Twenty Minutes

Before the IT companies, before any of it, I had seventy-five pounds left to my name. That was the whole of it. I was walking to the BBC at White City for an interview — spreadsheet operator — and I did not know what a spreadsheet was.

I passed a little shop that taught Excel and Word. I went in and asked the price. Seventy pounds for the course. My mind started doing the sums: seventy-five minus seventy left five. Five pounds for food until payday. The interview was in twenty minutes.

"If you can teach me in the next twenty minutes how to do Excel, I'll get the job, and I'll come back and pay for the full course."

The owner agreed. Twenty minutes later I walked into one of the most daunting buildings in London with my stomach in knots and just enough to get through the door. I got the job. Then I walked straight back to the shop, handed over the seventy pounds, and lived on five until I was paid.

You have to believe. Quite often you have to get all the way to the bottom first — and that is exactly where the biggest steps happen.

## Chapter 2 — The First Person I Hired

Two years in, the work was more than I could carry alone. Hiring someone felt impossible. The money was tight and the responsibility frightened me more than the workload.

His name was David. He had less experience than I wanted and more nerve than anyone I had met. I gave him a desk, a phone, and a fortnight to prove me right. He proved me right in a week.

What I learned is that you cannot scale fear. You can only scale trust. The moment I stopped doing everything myself was the moment the business started becoming a business.

You have to believe. I had said it to myself in a shop doorway years before, and here it was again, the same sentence doing the same work in a different room.

## Chapter 3 — Down to the Wire

There is a particular kind of cold that comes with an empty bank account. I have felt it more than once. The sharpest time was right at the start, before any of the companies existed.

I had seven hundred and fifty pounds to my name and a job interview at the BBC for a role running spreadsheets. The trouble was simple: I had never used a spreadsheet in my life. On the walk in, I passed a training shop, went inside, and asked what a course cost. Seventy pounds. I worked out what I would have left and made the man an offer — teach me enough in twenty minutes to land the job, and I would come back and pay in full.

He did. I walked into that building knowing almost nothing and walked out with the job. Then I returned and paid for the course.

The lesson has never left me. When you back yourself, the door tends to open.
