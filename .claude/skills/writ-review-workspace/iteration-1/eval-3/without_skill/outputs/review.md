# Copy Review — AI Consultancy Website Section

## The honest headline

This copy is grammatically clean and inoffensive, and that's exactly the problem. It reads like it could belong to any one of ten thousand consultancies. Nothing in it is yours. A prospect skims it, nods vaguely, and forgets it before they've reached the contact form. Right now it's wallpaper, not selling.

Below: what's working, what's holding it back, and a rewrite you can ship.

---

## What's working

- **It's clear and correct.** No jargon soup, no broken sentences, no over-claiming. That's a real floor to build on — plenty of copy fails even this.
- **The logic is sound.** AI → efficiency → competitiveness → talk to us. The skeleton of a persuasive argument is here; it's just wearing a beige suit.
- **There's a clear CTA.** "Free consultation" is a fine, low-friction ask.

---

## What's not working

**1. It's all "we," almost no "you."**
The first thing a reader subconsciously checks is "is this about me?" This copy opens with "At our company, we believe…" — the weakest possible start. It talks about what *you* do and believe far more than what the *reader* gets. Count the pronouns: it's heavily seller-centric.

**2. Every claim is a category cliché.**
"Improve efficiency and reduce costs," "streamline workflows," "automate repetitive tasks," "free up teams to focus on higher-value work," "stay competitive in a fast-moving marketplace." These aren't wrong — they're just the exact phrases every competitor uses. Interchangeable language signals an interchangeable provider. The reader's pattern-matcher files you under "generic" and moves on.

**3. Zero specificity, zero proof.**
"We have worked with many clients across a wide range of sectors" is the tell. *Many*, *wide range*, *measurable results* — all unfalsifiable filler. There's no number, no named outcome, no industry, no example, no timeframe. Vague claims of proof are read as the absence of proof. One concrete result ("cut a 12-person ops team's reporting time by 9 hours a week") would do more than the entire current draft.

**4. No differentiation — no reason to pick *you*.**
Nothing here answers "why this firm and not the other five I'm comparing?" There's no point of view, no method, no enemy, no opinion. "Our approach is to help business owners understand which tools are right for them" describes literally every AI consultant. What do you believe that others don't?

**5. It states benefits instead of making the reader feel a problem.**
"Efficiency is the key to staying competitive" is an abstraction floating in mid-air. It never names a moment of pain the reader recognises — the report that eats every Friday, the hire they can't justify, the competitor who quotes in minutes while they take days. Specific tension sells; abstract benefit doesn't.

**6. Flat, low-energy rhythm.**
Three paragraphs of similar length and cadence, each a polite, even-toned statement. No short punchy line, no contrast, nothing that makes the eye stop. The prose has no pulse.

**7. The CTA is weak and risk-free in the wrong way.**
"Please get in touch to arrange a free consultation" is passive ("please get in touch") and gives the reader no reason to act *now* and nothing concrete to expect. What happens on the call? What will they walk away with even if they don't buy?

---

## How to fix it — the priorities

1. **Flip to the reader.** Lead with their world, their problem, their goal. Make "you" the subject of most sentences. Demote "we."
2. **Replace one cliché with one specific.** Every generic claim should be swapped for a concrete number, named outcome, or recognisable scenario. If you can't be specific, cut the claim.
3. **Add proof.** At least one real result with a number. If you can't share figures, use a concrete before/after or a named sector and the change you drove.
4. **Take a position.** Say one thing your competitors wouldn't. A point of view is what makes a reader remember you.
5. **Open on tension, not on yourself.** Start with the problem the reader feels, not "At our company, we believe."
6. **Make the CTA concrete and forward.** Tell them exactly what they get and what to expect. Lower perceived effort, raise perceived payoff.
7. **Vary the rhythm.** Use a short, sharp line to create a stop. Let one sentence land hard.

---

## Rewrite (illustrative — swap in your real specifics)

> **Your team is spending hours on work a machine should be doing.**
>
> Reporting that eats every Friday afternoon. Data copied by hand from one system to another. The same questions answered in email a hundred times a month. It adds up — and while you're stuck doing it, faster competitors are quoting, shipping, and answering customers before you've finished the admin.
>
> We help business owners find the two or three AI tools that will actually move the needle — and we set them up, properly, so they pay off in weeks, not "someday." No 40-tool wishlist. No science projects. Just the handful that earn their keep.
>
> Recent example: we cut one 12-person operations team's weekly reporting from 11 hours to 90 minutes — inside a month.
>
> **Book a free 30-minute audit.** You'll leave with the three highest-impact automations for your business, mapped out — whether or not you work with us.

Notes on the rewrite:
- Opens on the reader's pain, in concrete images, not "we believe."
- Names a real (placeholder) number for proof — replace with a true one.
- Stakes a position ("two or three tools," "no 40-tool wishlist") that implies an enemy: over-engineered, never-shipped AI projects.
- The CTA promises a specific takeaway and removes risk ("whether or not you work with us").
- Mixes a punchy opener with longer explanatory lines to give it rhythm.

---

## One-line verdict

The bones are fine; the copy is just generic, seller-centric, and unproven. Make it about the reader, swap every cliché for a specific, add one real number, and stake a position — and it goes from forgettable to convincing.
