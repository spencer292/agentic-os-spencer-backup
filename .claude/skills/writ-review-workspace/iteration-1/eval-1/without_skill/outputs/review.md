# Review: Book Opening

## The piece

> I was born in Bulawayo in 1972. I went to school there, and later emigrated to the UK in 1997. I started my first IT company in 2001, and by 2008 I had three businesses employing around forty people between them.
>
> I worked long hours, typically twelve to fourteen a day, six days a week. In 2021 I experienced a period of burnout. After that I discovered breathwork and nervous system regulation, and I changed the way I worked. Revenue stayed roughly the same while my hours came down considerably.
>
> Today I coach other entrepreneurs on how to avoid the mistakes I made. This book is about what I learned along the way, and how the right use of AI can give business owners back their time.

## Verdict

This is not yet a strong opening. It is honest, clear, and competently written — but it reads as a CV in prose, not a book opening. It tells the reader the shape of a life without ever putting them *inside* a moment of it. A reader skimming the first page in a bookshop or on a Kindle sample has no reason to turn to page two: nothing has been promised, nothing is at stake, and the most dramatic event in the whole passage — burnout — is dispatched in nine words ("In 2021 I experienced a period of burnout"). The raw material is genuinely good. The delivery is flat.

## What's working

- **Clarity and economy.** Every sentence is easy to read. No throat-clearing, no purple prose, no jargon. That is a real asset and worth protecting.
- **A true arc exists underneath.** Striver → success → collapse → reinvention → teaching others. That is a proven, resonant memoir shape. The bones are sound.
- **A credible, specific premise.** "AI can give business owners back their time" is a concrete promise tied to lived experience, not a vague self-help platitude. The author has earned the right to make it.
- **Restraint.** It doesn't oversell or melodramatise. The understated tone is likeable and could become a strength once there's something underneath it to feel restrained *about*.

## What's not working

1. **It summarises instead of dramatises.** The whole passage is told from 30,000 feet — dates, headcounts, hours-per-day. There is not one scene, one image, one sensory detail, one line of the author actually *in* a moment. We are told a man burned out; we never see it.

2. **The biggest moment is buried and under-weighted.** Burnout is the hinge of the entire book and it gets less space than the company-formation dates. The emotional center is treated as a line item. This is the single most damaging structural choice here.

3. **Chronological order is the wrong order.** Starting with "born in 1972" is the default autobiography move, and it's the weakest possible entry point. The reader has no reason to care about 1972 yet. Openings should start where the *tension* is, not where the *timeline* is.

4. **No stakes, no reader, no promise in the right place.** The hook — what the book will give the reader — arrives only in the final sentence, almost as an afterthought, framed around the author ("what I learned") rather than the reader ("what you'll be able to do"). By the time the value proposition lands, an unconvinced reader has already left.

5. **Flat, additive sentence rhythm.** Many sentences share the same "I did X, and then Y" construction joined by "and." The cadence is monotonous, which makes even the interesting facts feel like a list. "Revenue stayed roughly the same while my hours came down considerably" — the most quietly powerful fact in the piece — is phrased like a footnote.

6. **Hedging language drains the punch.** "around forty," "roughly the same," "considerably," "a period of." These softeners are fine in conversation but they sand the edges off the very claims that should hit hardest. Precision is more persuasive: name a real number, a real loss, a real before-and-after.

## How to sharpen it

**1. Open in the scene of the collapse, not the year of birth.** Drop the reader into a concrete moment — the morning the author couldn't get out of the car, the email he couldn't bring himself to open, the realisation at a specific desk on a specific evening. Lead with the breaking point, then loop back to fill in how he got there. The 1972–2008 résumé can be compressed into two sentences *after* you've earned the reader's attention.

**2. Make burnout the centre of gravity, not a line.** Give it the most space, the most specific detail, and the most honest emotion of any moment in the opening. This is what the reader will recognise themselves in. Right now it's the one paragraph that should slow down and it's the one that goes fastest.

**3. Convert the strongest fact into the hook.** "Revenue stayed roughly the same while my hours came down considerably" is, quantified, the entire promise of the book. Make it precise and lead toward it: e.g., "I cut my working week roughly in half, and revenue didn't move." Sharpen the number, then build the page so it lands as a turn, not a footnote.

**4. Turn the premise toward the reader.** Recast the final move from "this book is about what I learned" to a clear promise of what the reader will be able to *do* — get their evenings back, stop trading hours for money, use AI to reclaim the time they're currently burning. Author-credibility first, reader-payoff second, both made explicit.

**5. Break the rhythm.** Vary sentence length deliberately. Let a very short sentence land a hard fact ("Then I collapsed.") after a longer build. Cut most of the "and"-joins.

**6. Replace hedges with specifics.** Swap "around forty," "roughly," "considerably," and "a period of burnout" for exact figures and one or two vivid, true details. The more specific the fact, the more the reader trusts it — and the harder it hits.

## One-line takeaway

The life is interesting; the page isn't yet. Stop narrating the timeline and start in the moment that broke you — then earn your way back to the dates. Lead with the collapse, dwell on it, and turn the closing promise to face the reader.
