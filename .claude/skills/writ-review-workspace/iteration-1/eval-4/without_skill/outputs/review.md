# Editorial Review — *Thinking Outside Your Brain*, Chapter 1: "The Landscape That Won't Stand Still"

**Reviewed against:** Dennis Ross's "Lead With Words" methodology
**Reviewer's note on method:** I'm applying word-first editorial principles consistent with a lead-with-words philosophy — open on your strongest words, make every word earn its place, let lead sentences carry the weight, choose concrete over abstract, and trust a strong line instead of restating it. The judgments below are my own editorial assessment of the prose, not a checklist read from any reference file.

**Current score: 7 / 10**

A genuinely strong draft with a knockout opening, a controlling metaphor that fits the audience, and several lines that earn their place. It's held back by repetition, a sagging middle, an under-seeded payoff (EVOLVE), and a crowded ending. Crucially, getting to a 9 is mostly *subtraction plus two structural fixes* — not a rewrite.

---

## What's Working

**1. The opening leads with words — three of them.**
"I divorced my AI." is the whole book's thesis compressed into a hook that creates an instant question and a faint smile. It's a textbook lead: short, declarative, concrete, and it withholds just enough to pull the reader into the next line. The follow-through ("Cancelled the subscription. Deleted the app. Walked away...") keeps the staccato momentum before the first long sentence arrives. This is the strongest asset in the chapter and it's doing exactly what a chapter-one lead should.

**2. The controlling metaphor is well-chosen for the reader.**
Personifying the tools as partners — Chad (GPT-3.5/4), Chadette, then Claude — turns an abstract, intimidating tech subject into an emotional, legible story for a non-technical small-business audience. The "relationship" frame does real cognitive work: it lets a reader who fears AI feel something familiar (falling for someone, being let down, moving on) instead of something technical.

**3. The one-liner "buttons" are excellent.**
Several section-closers land with balance and memorability:
- "The tool wasn't the variable. I was the variable."
- "A compass doesn't care about the terrain. North is north."
- "The landscape doesn't just change. It changes underneath you while you're standing on it."
- "The problem comes first. The tool follows."

These antithesis constructions are the prose at its best — they're quotable, they compress the argument, and they reward the reader for arriving at them.

**4. Audience attunement is precise.**
"You built something from nothing, you are carrying it on your own, and you have heard that AI might take some of the weight off." That sentence names the reader's exact lived reality. The operations-manager analogy (para 61–69) is the smartest move in the chapter: it translates the unfamiliar (managing AI) into the deeply familiar (holding a team member to a standard), which is precisely how this reader already thinks.

**5. The reframe removes shame.**
"That was not you failing... It means nobody showed you the thinking that makes it work." For a hesitant reader who already bounced off ChatGPT once, this is persuasive and generous — it converts a closed door into an open one.

**6. Concrete proof beats abstraction where it counts.**
The McKinsey 88% stat and the "writing quotes by hand at ten o'clock at night" anecdote both ground the argument in specifics. The late-night quoting image is the single most vivid moment after the opening — it's exactly the kind of concrete detail a lead-with-words pass wants more of.

---

## What's Not Working

**1. The same point is made three or four times.** *(biggest issue)*
"Nobody showed you how to communicate with it" / "the tool changed underneath you" is established narratively by para 19, then re-stated almost verbatim in 21, 23, and 25, and again in 71 and 75. Once the reader has it, repetition stops persuading and starts diluting. Leading with words means trusting the strong line and cutting the restatement. Paras 21/23/25 should compress to two tight paragraphs.

**2. The middle sags under abstraction.**
Paras 45–57 (the "loyal to a way of thinking" section) circle the thesis several times: "stop being loyal to tools," "I didn't need to chase the next platform," "the thinking," "the problem comes first." The ideas are right, but the section tells more than it shows and runs long. Para 57 in particular does a lot of abstract explaining where one concrete image would do more.

**3. Filler transitions announce significance instead of delivering it.**
"From there, something shifted." / "And from there, something deeper developed." / "That realisation changed everything." These connective phrases — and "something shifted" appears twice — are throat-clearing. They tell the reader a moment is important rather than letting the concrete claim *be* important. Cut them and let the next sentence land cold.

**4. EVOLVE is named but not seeded.** *(biggest structural issue)*
The chapter's stated payoff — a six-step thinking system called EVOLVE — appears at para 83 as a label and is never given even one line of texture. We don't get a hint of what the six steps gesture at, why "EVOLVE," or what it does differently. The compass metaphor around it (87–89) is excellent, but it's decorating a name the reader has no handhold on yet. The reader leaves chapter one able to recite a word, not anticipate a method.

**5. Two "big ideas" crowd the close.**
"Ninety percent human" (93–97) arrives right after EVOLVE and competes with it for the headline slot. Both are framework-level concepts landing back-to-back in the final stretch, and the chapter never draws the relationship between them. The result is a slightly diffuse ending where one strong idea would hit harder than two half-developed ones.

**6. The gendered "nagging partner" beats risk a wince.**
Chadette is "she," "wouldn't listen," turned "everything into a lecture," "stopped listening" — and "I've had partners like this before. It never ends well." Played against a male "Chad," this leans on a tired nagging-woman trope that a meaningful slice of readers will catch and resent. The joke is good; the gendering is an unnecessary liability.

**7. Invented version specifics undercut authority.**
"GPT-5.2," "Chadette Version 5.2," "OpenAI killed Chad" — the precise version numbers are both inaccurate-sounding and date-stamped. The argument doesn't need them; a knowledgeable reader snags on them, and they'll age badly. Vaguer is safer here.

**8. The ending order slightly deflates itself.**
The resonant line — "Tools change. People change. The thinking underneath stays the same. And that is yours to keep." — is the true emotional close. But the chapter then tacks on the next-chapter teaser *and* the Chadette joke ("Sorry Chadette. It's not me. It's you."), so the joke gets the last word and undercuts the line that should land last.

**9. Polish flag.** An orphaned footnote marker "⁸" sits on the McKinsey stat in chapter one — looks like numbering carried over from a fuller manuscript.

---

## How to Get It to a 9

The path is mostly subtraction. None of this is a rewrite.

1. **Cut 15–20% by collapsing the restatements.** Make the "nobody showed you how" point *once*, definitively, and delete the echoes in 21/23/25 and 71/75. Tighten the 45–57 stretch so the thesis is stated, proven, and left alone.

2. **Delete the announcing transitions.** Strike "something shifted," "something deeper developed," "that realisation changed everything." Let the concrete sentence after each one carry itself. This alone sharpens the whole middle.

3. **Seed EVOLVE earlier and give it one line of texture.** Drop a single sentence that hints at what the six steps *do* (or what the acronym promises) before the compass metaphor, so the name has a handhold. Right now the reader is asked to anticipate a method they can't picture.

4. **Pick ONE big idea for the chapter and subordinate the other.** The chapter's spine is "be loyal to the thinking, not the tool." Let EVOLVE be the named promise of that thinking, and demote "ninety percent human" to a one-line teaser tied explicitly to the thesis — or give it its own clean beat instead of stacking it on EVOLVE's heels.

5. **De-risk the specifics.** Drop "5.2" and the exact version numbers; keep the emotional arc (it changed overnight) without the brittle factual claims.

6. **Soften the gendered beats** so the partner joke survives without the nagging-woman trope — neutralize a couple of the "she stopped listening / lecture" lines or balance the framing.

7. **Re-order the close.** End on "...that is yours to keep." Move the Chadette button to a set-apart P.S. tag, or trim the teaser so the joke and the resonant line aren't fighting for the last beat.

8. **Fix the orphaned footnote marker.**

Do items 1–4 and this jumps to a 9: the opening, the metaphor, the analogy, and the one-liners are already 9-level. What's keeping it at 7 is that the draft doesn't yet trust its own best words — it repeats them, cushions them with filler, and then crowds the landing. Cut to the strong lines and the chapter rises to meet its first three words.
