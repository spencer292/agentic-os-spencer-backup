I still have the voicemail. Eleven seconds of nothing — a dial tone where Mike's voice should have been — left on the evening the company I'd spent three years building stopped being mine.

I'd driven to Leeds that morning to sign. Two hundred and forty grand, a two-year retainer, my name already typed beside his on the contract. By four o'clock the deal that was supposed to change everything had gone somewhere I couldn't follow, and Mike — who'd sat across from me at a dozen dinners, who knew my kids' names — had stopped answering his phone.

I called him from the car park. I called him from the motorway. I called him from my own driveway with the engine off and the rain coming down, telling myself the next ring would be the one that connected. None of them were.

Here's the part I've never said out loud, the part that still costs me. I'd seen it coming. For weeks the numbers had been soft and I'd known it, and I'd signed the office lease anyway, because admitting it felt worse than ignoring it. The collapse didn't blindside me. I'd helped build it.

That night I didn't sleep. I lay in the dark and did the only thing left to do — the math. Three years. Gone. Just like that.

Morning didn't fix anything; it only arrived. I made coffee I didn't drink and opened the laptop, and then, because there was genuinely nothing else, I emailed the one client I'd always been too proud to chase and asked for twenty minutes. He gave me ten. It was enough to start over.

I could end this the way these stories usually end — never quit, keep going, the words on the poster. But you already own those words, and not one of them has ever gotten anyone out of bed.

So here's the only thing worth handing you. The question that wrecked me at three in the morning wasn't *why did Mike do this to me.* It was the harder one: *what had I already known, and chosen not to look at?* That's where your rebuild starts too — not with the thing that was done to you, but with the part you can't pin on anyone else.
