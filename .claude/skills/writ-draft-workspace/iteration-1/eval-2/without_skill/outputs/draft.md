# The Day I Stopped Doing Everything Myself

It was a Tuesday night, and I was still at the office, doing the invoices.

Not reviewing them. Not approving them. Doing them — line by line, the way I had for years, even though by then we had people on the payroll who could have done it in half the time and probably with fewer mistakes. The building was dark except for my desk. Everyone else had gone home hours ago, to dinners and kids and whatever a normal evening looks like.

I'd been promising myself I would let go of this. Soon. Once things calmed down. That was the deal I'd struck with myself, and I'd been renewing it, quietly, for longer than I want to admit. The trouble is that things never calmed down. There was always another quote to finish, another fire, another job that felt too important to hand off. "Once things calm down" turned out to be a place you never actually arrive.

That night I missed something I'd promised my daughter I'd be at. I won't dress it up — I was at my desk finishing a quote for a client while it happened without me. The quote got done. It was a good quote. And I sat in the quiet afterwards feeling sick, because I knew exactly what I'd traded for it, and I knew it wasn't the first time.

Here's the part that should have made it obvious sooner. That year, the business turned over around £1.2 million. By any reasonable measure, it was working. We were busy, we were growing, the numbers were the kind of numbers I'd have killed for when I started. And the man running it was sitting alone at nine o'clock on a Tuesday typing invoices, because somewhere along the way he'd decided that if a thing was going to be done right, he had to be the one doing it.

That belief had built the company. It was now the thing holding it back. And it took me far too long — months of half-measures and good intentions — to see that those were the same belief wearing two different coats.

The shift didn't happen all at once. It started that Tuesday, but it took a long while to really take hold, because the hardest part wasn't the systems or the hiring or the handing-over. The hardest part was admitting the one thing I'd spent years arranging my life to avoid admitting:

The business didn't need me to do everything. It needed me to build the thing that does it without me.

If you're reading this, I suspect you already know which Tuesday I'm describing — because you've had one of your own, or you're having a run of them right now. You're the owner who's also the bottleneck. The one working twelve, fourteen hours a day and calling it commitment. The one who can't quite let go, because letting go feels like the same thing as not caring.

I'm not going to tell you it's easy, and I'm not going to pretend I had it figured out. I didn't. But I can tell you where it starts, and it isn't with a new tool or a new hire.

It starts with the night you finally admit you can't keep being the answer to every question.
