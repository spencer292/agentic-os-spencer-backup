The invoices could have waited until morning. We had people who could have done them, people I paid to do exactly that, and I sat there doing them myself anyway, the way I had every other week that year.

It was late, and it was a Tuesday. The office had emptied out hours before, and the only thing left in the building was me, a keyboard, and the quiet, self-righteous hum of being the last one still working. I called that commitment. I told myself I would hand it all off once things calmed down.

Things never calmed down. That is the part nobody warns you about. "Once it calms down" is not a date on the calendar. It is a horizon, and a horizon keeps its distance no matter how hard you walk toward it.

Picture the neck of a bottle. Everything inside, every drop the business is trying to pour into the world, has to pass through the same narrow opening. It does not matter how full the bottle is. The neck sets the speed. That night, the neck was me. Every quote, every invoice, every decision worth more than loose change funnelled down to one person at one desk, and the business poured exactly as fast as I could pour and not one drop faster.

I had understood the cost of that in the abstract for a long time. Then it stopped being abstract.

That evening my daughter had [[NEEDS: which of your daughter's events you missed that night]], and I was not there. I was finishing a quote for a client who, I can promise you, does not remember it today. I remember it. I felt sick.

It was not the dramatic kind of sick. It was the quiet kind, the kind that sits under your ribs while you keep typing anyway, because the quote still has to go out, because if you do not do it, who will. That question, *if I don't, who will*, had quietly run my life for years. That night it finally ran out of road.

On paper, we were a success. We turned over about £1.2 million that year, with [[NEEDS: how many of you there were that year — the team size]] of us holding it together. From the outside it looked like a man who had built something. From the inside it was a man who had built himself a job. A very expensive, very fragile job, with one employee who could never be replaced and was never once allowed to rest. Me.

The shift did not happen that night. It never does. It took [[NEEDS: how many months the shift took to fully take hold]] before the new way of working really held. But it started there, at that desk, over a quote I would give a great deal not to have finished, with a sentence I finally let myself believe instead of merely nod at:

The business does not need me to do everything. It needs me to build the thing that does it without me.

You probably already know which desk is yours. You know the night, the task you finished that could have waited, the face that was not looking back at you because you were not there to be seen. You have said the same thing to yourself. Once it calms down. There is a fair chance you are saying it tonight.

So before we go any further, here is one honest question, and you do not owe the answer to me, only to yourself. When your business keeps you at that desk after everyone else has gone home, ask what it is really asking you for. Whether it is asking you to do the work. Or whether it is asking you, at last, to build the thing that does the work without you.
