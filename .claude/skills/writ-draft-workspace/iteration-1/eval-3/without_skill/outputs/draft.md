# Chapter 1: The Landscape That Won't Stand Still

I divorced my AI.

I cancelled the subscription, deleted the app, and walked away from the tool I'd built my entire workflow around — the thing that had genuinely changed how I thought about my business — because it had stopped being the thing I fell in love with.

I need to tell you about that love story, because it explains everything that follows.

And I'll tell you where it's heading, so you're not reading blind. Everyone in this field hands you a map: which tool is best this month, which prompt to copy, which feature launched yesterday. The trouble is that maps of AI go out of date the week they're drawn. What I walked away with, in the wreckage of that relationship, wasn't a better map. It was a compass — and a compass doesn't care what the terrain underneath it is doing.

But the compass came later. First came Chad.

---

Back in 2023, I met Chad. GPT-3.5. It was love at first prompt.

He opened my eyes to what was possible. I'd ask a question and get something back that made me think differently — not perfectly, not always accurately, sometimes completely wrong. It didn't matter. For the first time I had something that could keep up with the way my brain works. I think fast, I talk fast, I jump between ideas, and Chad could handle all of it. We'd go back and forth for hours, and I'd come out the other side with thinking I couldn't have reached on my own.

Then Chad evolved into GPT-4 and we got closer. The quality jumped, the reasoning improved, and I started leaning on it for real work — client strategies, content, the operational thinking that used to eat my evenings. This wasn't a novelty anymore. It was a genuine shift in how I worked.

Then OpenAI killed Chad.

They replaced him with Chadette. Version 5.2. And I tried to make it work, I really did. But Chadette wouldn't listen. I'd ask for a paragraph and get an essay. I'd give clear instructions and watch her do her own thing entirely. Everything became a lecture. I've had partners like this before, and it never ends well.

What got me was how sudden it felt. One day it was the thinking partner I'd built my whole workflow around; the next day, after an update I never asked for, it was like talking to someone who had quietly stopped listening. The same questions I'd been asking for months came back different — longer, more generic, less like a conversation and more like a lecture. I hadn't changed. I was still bringing the same problems and asking in the same way. The tool had changed overnight, and it took the floor out from under me.

---

If you've tried AI and walked away thinking "this isn't for me," there are two reasons it probably happened.

The first — and this is the one that matters most — is that nobody ever showed you how to communicate with it. The instructions you give AI determine everything you get back, and most people have never been taught how to give a clear instruction. Not to AI. To anyone. This book will fix that.

The second is that the tool itself may have changed underneath you. AI platforms update constantly, and the thing you tried three months ago genuinely might not be the same thing you'd be using today.

So if you once opened ChatGPT, typed something about a proposal or a client email, got back something that could have come from anyone in your industry, and closed the tab thinking this isn't worth my time — that wasn't you failing. And the fact that you walked away doesn't mean you were wrong about the experience. It means nobody showed you the thinking that makes it work. That's what this book is for.

---

I've tested more AI tools since 2023 than any reasonable person should — hundreds of them — and the pattern repeats every single time. A tool launches, it's brilliant, it gets traction, the company scales, priorities shift, the product changes, and the thing you fell in love with becomes something you barely recognise. Writing tools, transcription tools, image generators, automation platforms: the cycle is always the same.

The landscape doesn't just change. It changes underneath you while you're standing on it.

---

So I started seeing Claude.

I felt guilty at first. But Claude listened. Claude understood what I needed before I'd finished explaining it, and I fell in love with AI all over again. For the first time in months I was shipping work faster than I could think of it. So I made it official — cancelled the ChatGPT subscription, divorced Chadette.

Now here's the honest part, and it isn't the part you're expecting. Yes, Claude will change too. Every tool does. The version I'm using today won't be the version in six months; something will update, some capability will shift, some competitor will do one thing better. I know, because I've lived the cycle enough times to see it coming.

But that's the easy honesty. Here's the harder admission, the one I don't enjoy putting in print.

For the better part of a year, I'd quietly handed my thinking over to a tool I didn't understand. I'd built client work on top of it and told people it was my edge — without ever once asking why it actually worked. So when Chadette stopped delivering, I had nothing underneath me. I missed a deadline I'd promised a client, and I spent that night rebuilding a strategy by hand, the same kind of strategy I'd been outsourcing for months, sitting there realising I'd somehow gotten worse at the one thing people pay me for. I'd sunk money into subscriptions I abandoned within weeks. I'd lost more Saturdays than I'll admit chasing the next tool that was finally going to fix everything. The tool hadn't made me sharper. It had made me dependent, and I didn't notice until it was gone.

That's the real reason the divorce mattered. Not because Chadette let me down, but because of what losing her showed me about myself.

The question was never whether the tools would change. They will. The question is what you do about it.

---

What I did about it was stop being loyal to tools and start being loyal to a way of thinking.

When Chad was brilliant, I didn't know why it worked — I just knew it did. When Chadette stopped working, I didn't know why either. I'd been reacting to the tool instead of understanding what was actually happening in the conversation, and that is precisely why losing it hurt so much: I had no idea which part of the magic had ever been me.

So I started paying attention to what made the good conversations good, regardless of which tool I was in front of. It came down to the same things every time.

When I was honest about what I didn't know, the output got better. When I was vague or lazy with my questions, the output came back vague and lazy to match. When I challenged what I was given instead of just accepting it, the thinking sharpened. And when I brought my own experience to the table — testing the AI's suggestions against more than two decades of doing the actual work — the result was something neither of us could have produced alone.

The tool wasn't the variable. I was the variable.

That one realisation changed everything. It meant I didn't have to chase the next platform every time something shifted; I needed to get better at the part that stayed the same — the thinking, the way I showed up to the conversation, the standard I held the AI to. And as the thinking improved, something else happened. I started knowing which tool to reach for before I reached for it. Not because I'd memorised every platform, but because I understood the problem well enough that the right tool became obvious. That's the real skill. A beginner grabs whatever is nearest and hopes for the best, while someone who has been through enough cycles knows what they need before they open anything. The problem comes first, and the tool follows. Once you understand that, every new tool that launches is something you can size up in minutes instead of losing a whole weekend to.

---

Think about how you manage your team. You don't just accept whatever your operations manager puts in front of you. You push back, you ask why, you hold them to a standard built on your own experience of what good looks like. And if they consistently underperform — if they stop listening, if the work stops matching what you need — you have a conversation. If the conversation doesn't land, you make a change.

That's exactly how AI works. Not technically. Practically.

You don't need to understand how a large language model processes tokens or how neural networks weight attention. You need to understand that a vague question gets you a vague answer, and that accepting the first thing the tool hands you without pushing back makes you a bad manager. When the tool stops serving you, you switch — no loyalty, no guilt, just pragmatism. I held Chadette to the same standard I'd hold anyone on my team. She didn't meet it, so I moved on. Claude meets it today. If Claude stops meeting it tomorrow, I'll move on again.

The fluidity is the skill, not the tool knowledge — the willingness to hold the standard, test the output, and change when you need to.

And none of it requires you to keep score. You do not need to know which tool launched last week or which platform updated yesterday; that was never your job. Your job is to understand the thinking underneath, because the thinking doesn't change when the tools do. If you've been feeling like you're falling behind because everyone's talking about AI and you don't even know where to start, you are not behind. You're about to start in the right place.

---

McKinsey's 2025 global survey found that eighty-eight percent of organisations are now using AI.⁸ Most have seen no real benefit from it, and I watch the reason play out constantly in the businesses I work with.

One owner asked ChatGPT to put together a quote for a new client. What came back could have been written for any company in the country — no mention of his approach, his standards, the way he actually delivers the work. He read it, said "this is useless," and went back to writing quotes by hand at ten o'clock at night. Nine times out of ten, it isn't the tool that fails; it's the question it was asked. He'd had a Chadette experience: the tool had changed, or the question was vague, or nobody had ever shown him how to think about the conversation. He wasn't wrong about the experience. He was wrong about the conclusion.

You're not reading this because you find technology interesting. You're reading it because you built something from nothing, you're carrying it on your own, and you've heard AI might take some of the weight off. You want to know whether that's real or whether it's one more thing that promises everything and delivers a slide deck. That's the right question — and the answer depends entirely on what happens between you and the tool, not on the tool itself.

The organisations getting real results aren't using better tools than everyone else. They're thinking about the work differently: holding AI to a standard, staying fluid when things change, and focusing on the principles underneath rather than the interface on top.

---

I told you at the start that what I walked away with was a compass, not a map. Here is what I meant. Every AI course, every tutorial, every "complete guide" is drawing a map of terrain that shifts every single month — so if you've been following maps, no wonder you've felt constantly lost. That isn't your fault. It's simply what maps do in a landscape that won't stand still.

A compass doesn't care about the terrain.

North is north.

EVOLVE is that compass — a six-step way of thinking that holds no matter which tool you happen to be using, because it's built on how humans think, not on how any one piece of software operates. It doesn't break when platforms update. It doesn't expire when the tool you love becomes the tool you leave. The rest of this book hands it to you one step at a time.

---

There's one more phrase you'll see throughout these pages: ninety percent human.

AI handles the heavy lifting; you do the heavy thinking. The research, the first drafts, the data — that's the tool's job. The strategy, the relationships, the judgement calls, the work you actually started your business to do before the admin and the quoting and the chasing swallowed everything — that's yours. And the more you work with AI this way, the more human your work becomes, not less, because you're finally free to bring your full self to the work that needs it instead of burning out on the work that doesn't.

The next chapter challenges how you think about your own brain — what it was built for, what it wasn't, and why the smartest move you can make right now is to stop trying to hold everything inside your head.

Sorry, Chadette. It's not me. It's you.

Tools change. People change. The thinking underneath them doesn't — and that, not the tool, is the thing worth getting good at. It's yours to keep.
