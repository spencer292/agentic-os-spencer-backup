## Part 1 — The Education Block (pulled verbatim from Spencer's closed calls)

Three ideas do the selling. They are not three separate facts — they are one argument with three moves, and Spencer runs them **before or alongside the price**, never after. The price lands as the logical answer to a problem the caller now understands.

> **The argument in one breath:** *Your yard is the food source → the tunnel is the net that catches the food → when the net stops producing, they dig new tunnel and you get new hills → moles are territorial, so when I catch this one another one takes the empty territory → it's not if they come back, it's when and how often → so you either pay me to come back four times a year, or I just stay.*

---

### Move 1 — The tunnel is a net to catch worms

This is the "why is this happening to me" move. It always comes first because it makes the caller feel understood instead of unlucky.

**Mark Brainard, 2026-08-04 (booked, TMCP):**
> "The way moles tunnels works is like a net to catch their food, which is worms. So they create the tunnel. The worms fall into the tunnel and the mole comes along and eats them. This time of the year we see the most activity because everything around us is drying out, and so the mole has to work harder to get enough food. They have to eat like 70% of their body weight every single day. And when the tunnel produces not enough worms, then the mole has to create new tunnels and you get new hills in your yard."

**Stavros Stratoudis, 2026-08-04 (booked, quick fix):**
> "Their tunnel works like a net to catch food and they have to create new tunnels in order to catch more food. And that's what you're seeing all these new hills for. Basically your yard is the food source."

**Kelsey Peck, 2026-07-21 (booked, TMCP):**
> "You put down brand new dirt, topsoil, it's fresh, it's healthy... and you've been watering a bunch. The moles eat worms and the worms have to be in that wet soil. So basically you've **rang the dinner bell** for the moles... They have to get rid of the dirt somewhere. And that's why you see all these mounds."

**The two lines that do the most work:**
- **"You're ringing the dinner bell."** — Spencer uses this on every caller who just re-sodded, re-seeded, or brought in topsoil. It reframes the yard from *victim* to *cause* without blaming them.
- **"Your yard is the all-you-can-eat buffet for moles."** (Erica Benson, 2026-07-20)

**The follow-on that kills the "they'll go away in winter" objection** — Will Reagan, 2026-07-23:
> "They don't actually go away. When it starts to rain, the moles don't have to make new tunnels, because the way their tunnels work is like a net to catch their food... In the winter, I catch moles year round on about a thousand properties. They're always active, we just don't necessarily see them. **It's a visibility thing.**"

Kelsey Peck, same objection, harder:
> "I'm gonna stop you. You *do* get the moles in the winter... You're thinking about molehills and not the whole problem. That's the wrong way to look at this. I catch moles before you ever get a molehill in your yard, all the time."

### Move 2 — Moles are territorial

This is the move that converts quick fix → annual. It answers "am I looking at 100 moles?" and "why will they come back?" in a single stroke.

**Mark Brainard, 2026-08-04:**
> "Most of the time they are isolationist, they're territorial. In most cases it's one or two moles that are causing all the damage at a time. **But because they do hold down these territories, another mole will move in to try and take it over. That's basically what this whole thing is about.**"

**2026-08-03 caller (Redmond):**
> "Yes, they're territorial. So what happens is I catch the moles and then another mole is going to find that tunnel. It's just a matter of *when* do they find it, not *if* they find it."

**Terence Wirth, 2026-07-27 (booked):**
> "Right now you have probably only one or two moles in the yard, and they're causing all that damage. Once I catch those moles, **it's a free-for-all for the next mole to come along and take those tunnels** — which they will, because you've got the food source. And there is really no way that I've ever found to get rid of worms. And you don't want to get rid of worms."

**The population-math version** (use it when they ask "will this ever end?") — Terence Wirth:
> "A female mole can give birth to like six to nine moles every single year. Let's say nobody in your area has been trapping them — you had six the first year, the next year you had thirty-something. And they are territorial, so they all push out. But once you put a serious dent in the population, it's going to take longer and longer for those moles to show up to take over the territory."

**And why we can trap without chemicals** — Mark Brainard:
> "They want to take the easy path. If they don't have to make a new tunnel, they don't... they just keep rerunning them over and over again, which is how we catch the moles. We don't use baits, chemicals, poisons, nothing. We are just using the patterning of what we know the moles do."

### Move 3 — It's not *if* they come back, it's *when and how often*

This is the pivot line. It is the sentence that makes the $100/month look like the sane option, and it is delivered as bad news honestly given, never as a pitch.

**Mark Brainard, 2026-08-04:**
> "The problem with moles is **it's not a question of if the moles are coming back, it's when and how often.**"

**Stavros Stratoudis, 2026-08-04:**
> "That is a temporary solution. It is, because moles are territorial and one mole will take over another mole's territory. It's really not a question of if the moles are coming back, it's when and how often."

**Duvall caller, 2026-07-24:**
> "There's no such thing as one-and-done mole control. It's really not a question of if they're going to come back, it's when. And sometimes it's one month and sometimes it's one year."

**Cindy Holshouser, 2026-07-21:**
> "Moles are active year round and they are territorial. And once you have moles, it's really not a question of if they're going to come back, it's when — or when you're going to get more."

**The honesty close** — Gregory Beckman, 2026-07-27 (booked next day):
> "The reason I've built that out is because most do come back. I can almost guarantee it. It's really not a question of if, it's when. I had so many people call me so many times throughout the year that I just developed that — because instead of calling me four or five times a year and paying me the 450 bucks, it was just more cost effective, and we had more success by staying out there year round."

**Kelsey Peck version:** *"The number of people who have called me one time ever is like very few."*

### The two supports that ride along with the three moves

**A. Reactive vs proactive** — this is what the $100/month actually buys, and it only makes sense *after* the net + territorial moves have landed:

> "We start by catching the moles that are causing the issues now, and then once we've caught those moles, we move all of our traps out to the perimeter of the property, and we locate the main runs where they're coming in, and we start trapping the moles **proactively instead of reactively**. In most cases, I'm catching moles before people even realize they have moles." — Stratoudis, 2026-08-04

**B. Spencer's own yard** — the proof line. It converts the pitch into a recommendation:

> "I trap the moles in my yard this way. I've got the same kind of situation as you — a couple of acres, I back up to the jungle and the moles just keep coming. **So the traps never leave the ground at my house.** And I'm terrible about checking them — my wife will say, when was the last time you checked the traps? And I'll have moles caught in the traps before I ever knew I had a mole." — Mark Brainard, 2026-08-04

> "This is how I do it at my house. I know the moles are always going to keep coming back, so I don't ever pull the traps out of my yard, I just keep moving them further out to the perimeter. I've been trapping there for five years and I'm still catching moles all the time — even before I ever get a molehill." — Terence Wirth, 2026-07-27

### Where the block sits in the call

This is the part that matters most for Muhammad. In every closed call this month, the order was:

1. Greeting → location → acreage → **"how long have you been dealing with the moles?"**
2. **Education block (all three moves, ~60–90 seconds, uninterrupted)**
3. *Then* both options, priced
4. Objections → answered with a callback to the block
5. Capture (name, cell, email, address, **"how did you find me?"**)
6. Quote by text + email → approve → schedule

The education block is not an answer to a question. It is a monologue Spencer volunteers before anyone asks, and it is why the price never has to be defended. On the Brainard call Spencer even flagged its length himself — *"I know, I got to take a drink after all of that, that was a lot of talk"* — and the caller bought the annual program.

> **Aside for Spencer:** both of the 2026-08-04 closes answered "how did you find me?" with **ChatGPT**. Brainard: *"I think ChatGPT sent us."* Stratoudis: *"I was doing ChatGPT, and ChatGPT said that you guys are rated."* That is now showing up often enough to be a real channel.

## Part 3 — Drills for Muhammad

### Drill A: The 60-second block, cold

Say this out loud ten times until it needs no thinking. Own words are fine; all five beats are not optional.

1. Moles eat worms, and they have to eat about **70% of their body weight every single day**.
2. Their tunnel system works **like a net** — the worms fall into the tunnels, the mole runs the tunnel and eats them.
3. This time of year everything around you dries out and **your watered lawn is the only food source left** — you've rung the dinner bell. When a tunnel stops producing enough worms, the mole digs new tunnel to catch more, and **that dirt has to go somewhere — that's your new hills**.
4. Moles are **territorial** — it's one or two moles doing all that damage, not a hundred. But that also means when we catch this one, **another one moves in to take the empty territory**.
5. So **it's not a question of if the moles come back — it's when, and how often.** That's why there are two ways to do this.

Then, and only then, the prices.

### Drill B: Three answers to memorize verbatim

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Do they go away in the winter?" | "They don't go away — we just stop seeing them. When it rains everywhere, the tunnels they already have produce enough worms, so they don't need to dig new ones, so you don't get new hills. It's a visibility thing. We catch moles year round on around a thousand properties." |
| "How many moles do I have — a hundred?" | "Probably one or two. They're territorial, so it's one mole at a time doing all of that. The catch is that when we take that one out, the territory is open and the next one moves in." |
| "Won't they just come back?" | "Yes. It's not if, it's when and how often. That's the whole reason the year-round program exists — instead of calling us four times a year at $450, we just stay out there for $100 a month." |

### Drill C: The capture list, out loud, one field at a time

He listed all four fields and then collected one. Ask them **one at a time and wait for the answer**:

**Name → cell → email → street address → "How did you find me?"**

That last one is not optional. It's how Spencer knows what's working — and right now it's telling him ChatGPT is sending customers.

### Drill D: Don't promise a day you haven't checked

Muhammad closed with *"we'll see you either today or tomorrow."* Nothing was checked. Spencer's version is *"I want to say Thursday, but let me make sure"* — then he looks. The address lookup tool answers this; use it before saying a day.
