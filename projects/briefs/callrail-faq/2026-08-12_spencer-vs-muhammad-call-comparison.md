# Spencer vs Muhammad — what the phone calls actually differ on

**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Method:** 47 of Spencer's inbound sales calls (CallRail, 2026-04-30 → 07-20, >90s, full transcripts)
against 28 of Muhammad's (2026-08-07, 08-10, 08-11). Structural signals extracted programmatically
across both sets; the two largest gaps were then verified by reading the transcripts directly rather
than trusting the pattern match.

---

## Bottom line

Muhammad is not losing calls on rapport, warmth, or credibility. On most measurable dimensions he
matches Spencer or beats him — he asks **more** qualifying questions, gets to the price at the same
point, and delivers the mole-biology education nearly as often and more accurately.

He is missing **one thing that decides the sale and one thing that colors it**:

1. **He does not close on the call.** Spencer moves to booking on **64%** of sales calls. Muhammad
   does on **17%**. Muhammad's close is "I'll send you the quote" — which hands the decision to an
   email, and the quote data shows what happens next.
2. **He speaks as "we". Spencer speaks as "I".** 69% of Spencer's pronouns are first-person
   singular against Muhammad's 48%. That is the entire mechanism behind what feels like rapport.

## The veteran theory is wrong — and the transcripts say so directly

"Veteran" or "military" appears in **6 of Spencer's 47 sales calls**. In **every single one**, Spencer
is asking whether the *customer* is a veteran, in order to give them a discount:

> "Are you a veteran by chance?" — *Jim Tewilliger call*
> "I do a veteran discount." — *Summit Drywall call*
> "Retired naval officer… I do a military discount, so I'm gonna knock some of this off." — *David Jones call*

He **never** offers his own service as a credibility play. Not once in 47 calls. Whatever is working,
it is not customers buying an American veteran's story — it's a discount-qualifying question, and
it comes up *after* the price, not as rapport-building at the top.

What Spencer actually leads with is **ownership**, and he does it constantly:

| | Spencer | Muhammad |
|---|---|---|
| First-person singular ("I", "my") | **69%** | 48% |
| Calls with an owner/experience claim ("I've been doing this 15+ years") | 20 of 47 | rare |

> "**I'm** not going to leave you with moles."
> "The only reason **I** wouldn't catch moles is if they're not in your yard."
> "For $3 a day, **I'll** trap the mole for the whole year."

Against Muhammad's:

> "**We'll** be able to catch the ones that you have now."
> "**We** have two options that you could go about."

Same facts. One is a person taking responsibility; the other is a company describing a service. That
is the "instant rapport" — it is transferable, and it has nothing to do with nationality or service record.

## What Spencer does that Muhammad doesn't

### 1. He recommends. Muhammad presents a menu.

Muhammad, near-verbatim on nearly every call:

> "So we have two options that you could go about. One is the quick fix, that's 450… And the other
> option is the total mole control program. That's $100 a month."

Then he stops and waits. The customer is left to do the comparing.

Spencer gives the same two options but argues for one the entire time — "my most popular option",
"it's not a chance they're gonna come back, it's just a question of when" — and keeps talking through
the price rather than pausing for a verdict.

### 2. He tells field stories. Muhammad recites facts.

Spencer, unprompted, mid-pitch:

> "I've got one right now where I showed up the first week, there was a bunch of molehills. I set the
> traps, the moles disappeared… and then two weeks later, the moles reappeared. So I moved everything
> to a different side of the yard and… guess what? The mole disappeared again on me. And I'm like,
> well, this is fun, we're playing cat and mouse here."

That one story does three jobs at once: proves he is personally in yards, pre-handles "what if it
takes longer than five weeks", and makes the leniency policy concrete before anyone can doubt it.

Muhammad's biology script is *more* accurate than Spencer's and he delivers it cleanly — but it is
textbook. He has no yard stories, because he has never been in a yard. **This is the one gap that
cannot be closed by drilling; it has to be supplied.**

### 3. He pre-empts objections. Muhammad answers them.

Every objection in Muhammad's calls is customer-initiated. He answers them accurately — cancel math,
deposit structure, trap safety, all correct. But he never raises one first.

Spencer volunteers the risk reversal before anyone asks for it:

> "You pay me at the end of the month. If I don't catch any moles and the problem goes away on its
> own, all I charge is the $150 setup fee. **The only reason I wouldn't catch moles is if they're not
> in your yard.** Otherwise I'm not going to leave you with moles."

That is the no-catch guarantee delivered as *confidence*, not as policy — which is why Spencer's
"guarantee mentioned" rate looks low (15%) while the reassurance is actually present everywhere.

### 4. He reframes the price. Muhammad states it.

- Spencer: **"For $3 a day, I'll trap the mole for the whole year."**
- Spencer: **"450 bucks flat rate. Doesn't matter if I catch one mole or 20 moles. Or 100 moles."**
- Muhammad: "It's $100 a month." / "It's 450 with 150 deposit."

Same numbers. One has been made to feel small; the other is just a figure.

### 5. The Jerome McDaniel call is the clearest single miss

8 minutes. The caller believed the price was $150 and had a previous mole guy who charged $100 a catch.
Muhammad corrected him three times, mechanically:

> "It's actually 450 and 150 is a deposit."
> "What happens is that our quick fix is 450. It's not 150."
> "No, no, no. That's two different plans."

He never asked what the old guy actually did, never reframed to flat-rate value, never sold the
difference between a per-mole handyman and a season-long program. Spencer's flat-rate line —
*"doesn't matter if I catch one mole or 20 moles, or 100 moles"* — is built precisely for this call
and was never deployed. The call ended nowhere.

## Why this joins up with the quote analysis

The 2026-08-12 quote study found Muhammad converts **82%** of the quotes customers actually open,
against Spencer's 81% — identical — but that his quotes get opened 58% of the time against Spencer's 89%.

This explains it. **Spencer's quote is paperwork for a sale he already made on the phone.** That is
why 30% of all won quotes were converted in Jobber *before the customer ever opened them* — they were
sold verbally. Muhammad's quote *is* the sales pitch, sent to an inbox, competing with everything else
in it. He is asking an email to do the job Spencer does with his voice.

His email-capture rate (50% vs 81%) compounds it: half his calls end without the one asset his chosen
close depends on.

## What to drill — three lines, in order

1. **"Let's get you on the schedule."** Not "I'll send you a quote." The quote goes out *after* a day
   is agreed. Every sales call ends with an attempted booking, and the quote is confirmation of it.
   This is the 64%-vs-17% gap and it is worth more than everything else combined.
2. **Switch "we" to "I".** "I'll get the traps set", "I'm not going to leave you with moles",
   "I'll take care of it." Same script, one pronoun. This is the rapport, and it is a mechanical change.
3. **Give him three field stories.** He cannot invent them and should not fake them. Spencer or Cory
   should hand him three real ones — the cat-and-mouse yard, a big catch, a property where the
   perimeter strategy stopped a recurring problem — with permission to tell them as the company's
   stories ("we had one last month where…"). Without these he will stay a well-informed brochure.

**Do not drill:** qualifiers, the block, the biology, or capture-of-name. The data says those are done —
he beats Spencer on discovery. Adding more there is wasted training time.

## Method / caveats

- Spencer's set spans 12 weeks; Muhammad's is 3 days. Spencer's is also self-selected toward calls he
  chose to take.
- Signal detection is regex over the agent's side of the transcript, so rates are approximations —
  phrasing outside the pattern is missed. The two headline gaps (booking rate, pronouns) were verified
  by reading full transcripts, not taken from the pattern match alone.
- "Asked for name" reads 15% for Spencer vs 78% for Muhammad. That is an artifact — Spencer usually
  has the name already from CallRail or an existing record. Not treated as a finding.
- Transcripts are machine-generated; "Got Moles" appears variously as "Gottwell", "out Moles",
  "Total Mold Control". Wording quoted above is as transcribed.
