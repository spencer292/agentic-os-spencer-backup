Flat rate is the better default for house cleaning: it rewards you for getting faster, not slower, and it lets clients book without negotiating a rate first. Hourly makes more sense for jobs you can't scope in advance: first-time deep cleans in an unknown house, or hoarding-level cleanups where square footage tells you almost nothing about the labor involved. Most operators run flat rate for the bulk of the book and hourly as the exception, not the other way around.

## What's the actual difference between hourly and flat rate?

Hourly bills for time worked; flat rate bills for the job, no matter how long it takes.

With hourly pricing, the client pays for however many hours the clean takes, at an agreed rate per hour (often per cleaner, sometimes per team). Run long because the fridge was disgusting? They pay for that time. Finish early because the house was already tidy? They pay less.

With flat rate, you quote one number based on square footage, clean type, and condition, and that number doesn't move once the job starts. Run over your estimated time and you eat it. Finish in half the time and you keep the difference.

The mechanics are simple. The incentives they create are not, and that's the part worth thinking through before you pick one for your business.

## Why do most cleaning businesses move to flat rate?

Because hourly pricing quietly punishes you for getting good at the job.

Once you or your team get efficient, an hourly model means faster work equals lower pay for the exact same clean. A three-hour house that used to take four hours now bills the client 25% less, for identical results. That's backwards. Flat rate flips the incentive: the faster and better your systems get, the more you keep per hour, without ever raising the client's price.

Flat rate also makes booking easier. A prospect calling for a quote wants a number, not a range with "depends how long it takes" attached. A firm flat quote closes faster than "somewhere between $150 and $300, we'll see."

| Factor | Hourly | Flat rate |
|---|---|---|
| Client experience | Uncertain final cost | Knows the price upfront |
| Reward for efficiency | None — faster work pays less | Full — faster work pays more per hour |
| Scheduling predictability | Low — job length varies | High — you can pack the route tighter |
| Risk on unknown jobs | Low for you, absorbed by client | High for you if you misjudge condition |
| Best fit | First cleans, unknown condition, restoration-level jobs | Recurring cleans, standard/deep cleans you've scoped before |

## When does hourly actually make more sense?

When you genuinely can't estimate the labor before you walk in.

A handful of situations where hourly protects you better than flat rate:

- **True first-time cleans in an unseen house.** You haven't walked the property, and the listing photos don't tell you if it's lightly dusty or a two-year backlog.
- **Hoarding or extreme-clutter jobs.** Square footage stops meaning anything when half the house is inaccessible until items get moved.
- **Post-construction or post-renovation cleanup.** Dust and debris volume varies wildly by contractor, and a flat quote here is a guess dressed up as a number.
- **Open-ended "help us get organized" requests.** If the client themselves doesn't know the scope, you can't price the scope either.

Outside of those, hourly mostly just transfers your estimating risk onto the client's bill, and clients notice when that risk runs long.

## How do you set a flat rate without guessing?

Start from a real production rate, not a number that sounds fair.

The formula is the same one behind a [house cleaning pricing calculator](/guides/house-cleaning-pricing-calculator/): square footage divided by how many square feet you clean per hour, times your target hourly rate, then adjusted for clean type and condition. If you don't have a production rate yet, time your next 10-15 cleans against square footage. Most standard cleans land in the 800-1,200 sq ft/hour range, slower for a first clean or a heavily soiled home.

The number you're protecting is your floor: the minimum you can charge and still cover labor, drive time, and supplies with margin left over. A flat quote that undercuts your floor because you misjudged the house is the real risk of this model, not a client who negotiates.

## Can you run both models at once?

Yes, and most established cleaning businesses do, just not for the same job type.

- [ ] Flat rate for standard recurring cleans (weekly, biweekly, monthly) where you've cleaned the house before
- [ ] Flat rate for deep cleans and move-outs you can scope from photos or a quick walkthrough
- [ ] Hourly (with a stated minimum) for true first-time cleans in unknown-condition houses
- [ ] Hourly for hoarding, extreme clutter, or post-construction jobs
- [ ] A published range on your [price list](/guides/cleaning-service-price-list-template/) even for hourly jobs, so prospects aren't calling in completely blind

The split isn't really a compromise. It matches the pricing model to how much you actually know about the job before you start it.

## How do you switch an hourly client to flat rate without losing them?

Quote the switch using their own history, not a fresh guess.

If you've cleaned a client's house on an hourly basis for a few visits, you already have the data a flat quote needs: average time per visit. Take that average, multiply by your rate, and that's your flat number, no different math than a brand-new flat quote, just backed by real hours instead of an estimate. Frame it to the client as simplification, not a price hike: "same clean, one predictable number instead of a variable bill." Most clients prefer that trade once it's explained, because the uncertainty was never their favorite part of hourly billing either.

## FAQ

**Is flat rate riskier for the cleaner than hourly?**
On any single unpredictable job, yes: you can underquote and eat the overage. Across a whole recurring book, it's usually lower risk overall, because you're pricing houses you already know and getting paid the same regardless of how efficient you get, which compounds in your favor over time.

**Do clients prefer hourly or flat rate?**
Most prefer flat rate because it removes the "how much will this actually cost" anxiety before booking. Hourly can feel fairer on paper, but in practice it just moves the uncertainty onto the client's final bill.

**Should first-time cleans always be hourly?**
Not always. If you can see photos or do a quick walkthrough and the house looks like a standard clean, flat rate still works. Hourly is for when the condition is genuinely unknown, not for every new client automatically.

**What's a fair hourly rate for house cleaning in 2026?**
Rates vary heavily by market, but most solo and small-team operators charge somewhere in the $35-$60/hour range per cleaner, with team rates scaling from there. Treat any number here as a starting range to test locally, not a fixed figure. See the full breakdown in [how much to charge for house cleaning](/guides/how-much-to-charge-for-house-cleaning/).

**Can I charge a flat rate but still track my hours internally?**
Yes, and you should. Tracking actual time against your flat quote is how you catch a production rate that's drifted, or a house that's consistently taking longer than the number you quoted, without ever changing what the client sees.
