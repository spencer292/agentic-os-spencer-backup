A solo residential cleaning business needs six categories of supplies to start: a vacuum, mopping system, caddy, microfiber cloths, general chemicals, and basic safety gear. Realistic 2026 US ranges run $150–$400 for a starter kit sourced from a big-box or restaurant-supply store. Anything beyond that, a backpack vacuum, a floor machine, a full commercial chemical line, can wait until the client list justifies it.

## What supplies do you actually need to start a cleaning business?

Fewer than most new owners guess. A working solo kit covers five jobs: pick up dirt, wipe surfaces, mop floors, carry everything, and protect yourself while doing it. Past that, it's mostly preference.

The mistake that shows up most in year-one budgets is buying commercial-grade equipment before there's commercial-grade work to justify it. A $400 backpack vacuum earns its keep across a route of twenty homes a week. It sits in a closet if you're doing four.

Most solo operators land closer to the low end of the starter range if they already own a vacuum and a mop. Buying everything new pushes toward $400.

## Should you buy eco-friendly or standard chemicals to start?

Either works functionally. The decision is more about positioning than performance. Eco-friendly and fragrance-free lines cost roughly 15-30% more per bottle but give you a clean answer when a client asks about kids, pets, or allergies, a question that comes up more often than new owners expect.

A workable middle ground: stock one eco-friendly all-purpose cleaner as the default, and keep a standard degreaser or bathroom cleaner on hand for jobs that need more cutting power. Nobody needs an entirely eco-friendly line to win their first clients, but having the option ready saves an awkward conversation.

## What should you skip when starting out?

Three categories of spending routinely outpace what a startup route needs:

- **Commercial floor machines** before you've booked a commercial contract that requires one
- **A full uniform order** (embroidered polos, matching vehicle decals) before you know the business will stick
- **Specialty chemical lines** for services you haven't been asked to perform yet (carpet extraction, tile and grout restoration)

None of these are wasted money forever. They're just money spent before the job that pays it back exists. Buy them when a client asks for the service, not in anticipation of one.

## Is it worth buying used equipment to save money?

For a vacuum, yes, within reason. A used upright or canister vacuum from a reputable seller can cut that line item in half, and vacuums are mechanically simple enough that a working used unit is a working used unit. Check the belt and filter before the first job either way; both are cheap to replace and both are the first things to fail on an older machine.

Chemicals are the opposite case. Never buy used or opened chemical containers. Dilution ratios and shelf life aren't worth guessing on, and a mislabeled or degraded bottle can damage a client's floor or countertop on the first job. Buy chemicals new, every time, no exceptions.

Caddies, totes, and cloths fall in between: fine used if they're clean and intact, not worth chasing a discount on since they're cheap new anyway. Save the used-equipment hunting for the higher-ticket items, vacuums first, floor machines later if the business grows into needing one.

## How do you keep supply costs under control after the first few months?

Track cost-per-job, not just total spend. A $12 bottle of concentrate that makes 32 diluted bottles of all-purpose cleaner costs closer to $0.40 per job than the sticker price suggests. Ready-to-use sprays feel cheaper at checkout and cost more per use once you're doing five houses a week.

Buying in bulk from a restaurant-supply or janitorial-supply store (rather than a standard retail aisle) usually cuts per-unit cost by a third or more once you're past the first kit. That's the point where most solo cleaners switch suppliers, not before.

Building out the paperwork side, a signed [service agreement](/guides/cleaning-business-contract-template/) and a [client intake form](/guides/cleaning-business-client-intake-form/), matters just as much as the physical kit. A well-stocked caddy and no signed agreement is still a business exposed to a no-show or a scope dispute on day one.

## Cleaning Business Startup Supply Checklist

- Upright or canister vacuum, ideally with attachments for stairs and upholstery
- Mop system: bucket and mop head, or a spray mop for smaller homes
- Caddy or tote sized to carry a full job's supplies in one trip
- 20-30 microfiber cloths, color-coded by room or surface
- All-purpose cleaner (concentrate, if you're buying more than a bottle or two)
- Glass cleaner, bathroom cleaner, and a wood-safe surface cleaner
- Gloves and knee pads
- A dust pan and hand broom for quick pickups between vacuum passes
- Trash bags sized for a range of client bin sizes
- A rolling cart or bag if you're carrying supplies up multiple flights regularly

Ten items, most sourced from one big-box trip or a single online order. None of it requires waiting on a business loan.

## FAQ

**Do I need commercial-grade equipment to start a cleaning business?**
No. Home-grade equipment handles the first several months of residential work fine. Commercial-grade gear (backpack vacuums, floor machines) pays for itself once route volume or commercial contracts justify the cost, not before.

**How much should I budget for cleaning supplies in my first month?**
$150-$400 covers a full starter kit for a solo residential operator, including a vacuum, mop system, chemicals, and basic PPE. Anyone who already owns a vacuum and mop can start closer to $100-$150.

**Should I buy eco-friendly cleaning products?**
Optional but useful for pitching pet- and kid-safe service. A one-line mix, a fragrance-free all-purpose cleaner alongside a standard degreaser, covers most client preferences without doubling your chemical budget.

**Where's the cheapest place to buy startup cleaning supplies?**
Big-box retail works fine for the first kit. Restaurant-supply and janitorial-supply stores typically undercut retail by a third or more on concentrates and bulk cloths once you're restocking regularly.

**What's the biggest supply mistake new cleaning business owners make?**
Buying commercial-grade equipment before there's commercial-grade demand. A floor machine or backpack vacuum sitting unused is money that could have gone toward marketing or insurance in month one.
