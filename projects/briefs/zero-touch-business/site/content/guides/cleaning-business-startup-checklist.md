Starting a cleaning business takes five things in place before the first job: a registered business entity, general liability insurance, a starter supply kit, a price list you can quote from without stalling, and the paperwork (contract, intake form, invoice) to make the first client feel like your fiftieth. Most of that can be done in a week for a few hundred dollars. The part that actually takes time is getting the pricing right.

## What do you need to start a cleaning business?

Five categories, roughly in the order you'll touch them: legal setup, insurance, supplies, pricing, and client-facing paperwork. Skip any one of them and it shows up as a problem later: an uninsured accident, a client who never signed anything, a quote you gave too low because you hadn't worked out your real numbers yet.

The good news: none of these five are hard on their own. The mistake most new owners make is doing them out of order: buying supplies and booking a first client before they've registered the business or worked out what to charge.

## How much does it cost to start a cleaning business?

Startup cost depends almost entirely on whether you're solo with your own supplies or building toward a crew from day one. Realistic 2026 US ranges, varies by market:

| Startup item | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Business registration (LLC or sole prop) | $0–$300 | Sole prop is often free; LLC filing fee varies by state |
| General liability insurance (annual) | $400–$800 | Bonding adds $100–$300/yr if you want it, and some clients require it |
| Starter supply kit (solo, residential) | $150–$400 | Vacuum, mop, caddy, eco-friendly chemicals, microfiber cloths |
| Commercial-grade equipment (if starting bigger) | $1,000–$3,000+ | Backpack vacuum, floor machine, larger chemical inventory |
| Scheduling/invoicing software | $0–$50/mo | Many tools have a free tier for solo operators |
| Marketing (flyers, Google Business Profile, basic site) | $0–$300 | GBP is free; a lot of first clients come from it and word of mouth |

Solo operators can realistically be quoting jobs for under $1,000 all-in. Anyone budgeting to hire from month one should plan closer to $3,000–$5,000.

## What licenses and insurance does a cleaning business need?

This varies by state and county — verify locally before you book anything, and this isn't legal advice. Most jurisdictions require some form of business registration (LLC or sole proprietorship) and a general business license from the city or county. Some states require a separate license for businesses handling chemicals commercially, though this is more common for larger commercial-cleaning operations than residential.

General liability insurance isn't usually legally required for a solo residential cleaner, but skip it at your own risk. One broken vase or water damage claim without coverage can wipe out a year of profit. Bonding (protects clients against theft claims) is optional but a real selling point for nervous first-time clients, especially anyone letting you into their home while they're out.

## What paperwork do you need before your first cleaning job?

Three documents cover almost every early-stage problem: a signed [service agreement](/guides/cleaning-business-contract-template/) that spells out scope, cancellation policy, and payment terms; a [client intake form](/guides/cleaning-business-client-intake-form/) that captures access instructions, pet info, and areas to avoid before you ever set foot in the home; and an invoice template so getting paid doesn't turn into an improvised text message every time.

None of these need to be complicated. A one-page agreement and a short intake form cover 90% of what goes wrong in a cleaning business's first few months: no-shows with no cancellation policy to point to, a locked-out crew because access instructions lived in someone's memory instead of on paper.

## How do you price cleaning jobs when you're just starting out?

Slowly, and by tracking your actual time. Quote your first handful of jobs using a [pricing calculator](/guides/house-cleaning-pricing-calculator/) based on square footage and condition, but log how long each job actually takes you. New cleaners routinely underprice because they estimate speed based on how fast they'll be once they've done a hundred houses, not the first ten.

A simple rule that holds up: price for the time it takes you now, not the time you expect to take once you're fast. You can lower prices as you speed up. Raising a price on an existing client is a much harder conversation than starting close to the real number.

## Cleaning Business Startup Checklist

Work through these roughly in order:

- Register the business (LLC or sole proprietorship) and get an EIN if you'll ever hire
- Open a separate business bank account — don't mix personal and business funds from day one
- Get general liability insurance quotes and bind a policy before the first paid job
- Check city/county licensing requirements for your specific service area
- Buy a starter supply kit sized to your first few bookings, not your five-year plan
- Build a price list or calculator based on square footage and job type
- Create a one-page service agreement and client intake form
- Set up an invoicing method (software or a simple template) before you need it
- Claim your Google Business Profile — it's free and it's where a lot of first clients find you
- Book your first 3-5 jobs at a rate you can defend, and track actual time spent on each

Ten items, most of them a single afternoon each. The whole list is doable inside two weeks without quitting a day job to do it.

## FAQ

**Do I need an LLC to start a cleaning business?**
No. A sole proprietorship is legal in every state and often free to set up. An LLC adds liability protection between your business and personal assets, which matters more once you're carrying insurance, hiring, or taking on commercial contracts. Many solo cleaners start as a sole prop and convert to an LLC in year one or two.

**How much money do I need to start a cleaning business?**
A solo residential operation can start for under $1,000 including insurance and a basic supply kit. Building toward a crew from the outset pushes that closer to $3,000-$5,000 for equipment and a larger chemical inventory.

**Can I start a cleaning business without experience?**
Yes, and most owners do. The learning curve is mostly about speed and pricing accuracy, not technique. Cleaning itself isn't hard to learn. The bigger early risk is underpricing because you don't yet know how long jobs actually take you.

**Do I need insurance before my first job?**
Strongly recommended, even though most areas don't legally require it for a solo residential cleaner. One incident without coverage — water damage, a broken heirloom, a slip and fall — can cost more than years of premiums.

**What's the fastest way to get my first clients?**
A free Google Business Profile plus word of mouth from friends, family, and neighborhood Facebook groups accounts for most new cleaners' first bookings. Paid ads and a full website can wait until you've validated pricing and workflow on a handful of real jobs.
