# Lawn Care Business Starter Kit
**File 14 — Seasonal Service Schedule & Checklists** | v1.0 | 2026-07-26

---

## How to use this

Print these, keep a set in the truck, and run the same list every visit. A checklist isn't an insult to a good crew — it's how the lawn looks identical whether it's you, your best guy, or someone on day three. Nearly every complaint in this trade traces back to one skipped line: an edge not cut, clippings left on a walk, a gate left open.

Two things to understand before the lists.

**The one-third rule governs everything.** Never remove more than one-third of the grass blade in a single cut. Cutting lower stresses the plant, exposes soil to weed seed, and produces the brown, scalped lawn that gets a company fired in July. If the lawn got away from you, cut it high, then cut it again in a few days — two cuts, not one scalping.

**Timing is regional.** The sequence below is correct everywhere; the calendar isn't. Cool-season grasses (fescue, bluegrass, rye) and warm-season grasses (bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine) want opposite things at opposite times — a fall overseed is standard for one and wrong for the other. **Confirm the timing for your grass type and region with your state's cooperative extension service** — it's free, it's local, and it's the best technical resource in this industry.

## CREW STANDARDS — Every Visit, Every Time

1. **Arrival:** Service on the scheduled day inside the window. Text on completion if that's your client's preference.
2. **Walk it first:** Thirty seconds. Pick up debris, sticks, toys, hoses, pet waste. **Anything you leave, the mower throws.**
3. **Gates and pets:** Note pets before opening anything. Close and latch every gate — this is the single most damaging mistake in the trade.
4. **Height:** Correct height for the grass type and season. One-third rule, no exceptions.
5. **Sharp blades:** Dull blades tear grass and leave a whitish-brown tip across the whole lawn. Sharpen on a schedule, not when you notice.
6. **Vary the pattern:** Alternate mowing direction each visit. Prevents ruts and grain, and it's what makes a lawn look professionally striped.
7. **Blow every hard surface:** Drive, walks, patio, porch, street gutter. Clippings on concrete are what the client sees from the window.
8. **Never blow clippings into the street drain** — it's a stormwater violation in many municipalities and it's how you get a call from the city. Verify locally.
9. **Photos:** Periodic after photos, especially on cleanups. Your marketing library builds itself.
10. **Leave it better:** Gates latched, equipment loaded, nothing left behind. The last look is what they describe to a neighbor.

# CHECKLIST 1 — STANDARD MOWING VISIT

*The recurring service. This is the product they pay for every week — it should look identical every time.*

## Before the mower moves

- [ ] Walk the property; clear sticks, stones, toys, hoses, pet waste
- [ ] Note anything new: damage, marked irrigation, standing water, bare spots
- [ ] Confirm turf is dry enough to cut without rutting
- [ ] Check blade height is correct for grass type and current conditions

## Mow

- [ ] Mow at the correct height; never remove more than one-third of blade height
- [ ] Alternate direction from the previous visit
- [ ] Overlap passes slightly — no mohawk strips between rows
- [ ] Slow down on turns to avoid tearing turf
- [ ] Mow around marked irrigation heads and concealed items with care
- [ ] Handle slopes and ditches with the appropriate equipment, or leave and note it
- [ ] Bag, mulch, or side-discharge per the client's agreement

## Trim and edge

- [ ] String trim all areas the mower can't reach: fence lines, foundations, posts, beds
- [ ] Trim carefully around trees — leave a collar rather than scar the bark
- [ ] Trim at the same height as the mow; scalped trim lines are a giveaway
- [ ] Edge walks, driveway, and curb — crisp lines are what separates "mowed" from "maintained"
- [ ] Edge bed lines per the client's agreement

## Finish

- [ ] Blow all clippings off drive, walks, patio, porch, and street
- [ ] Direct clippings back onto turf, never into drains or the neighbor's property
- [ ] Final look from the street — this is the view the client and every neighbor sees
- [ ] Gates closed and latched; pets secure
- [ ] Note anything the client should know (disease, insect pressure, irrigation issue, damage)
- [ ] Completion text/report sent before leaving

# CHECKLIST 2 — SPRING CLEANUP

*The season opener and one of your best-margin jobs. Sell it as a package, not an hourly.*

- [ ] Walk the property with the client if possible; set scope and expectations
- [ ] Remove leaves, needles, and winter debris from turf and beds
- [ ] Clear window wells, fence lines, and corners where debris collected
- [ ] Cut back ornamental grasses and perennials per plant type
- [ ] Prune winter-damaged and dead material (know what blooms on old wood before cutting)
- [ ] Edge and redefine all bed lines
- [ ] Weed beds before mulching — mulch over weeds is a callback in three weeks
- [ ] Apply pre-emergent if licensed and appropriate for the region and timing
- [ ] Install mulch to correct depth ({2–3 inches}); keep it off trunks — no volcano mulching
- [ ] Rake or dethatch turf where matting or snow mold is present
- [ ] Note bare spots, grub damage, vole runs, and drainage issues; quote repairs
- [ ] First cut at a height appropriate for the season
- [ ] Blow all hard surfaces
- [ ] Before/after photos — spring cleanups are your strongest visual sell
- [ ] Present the recurring maintenance agreement while the property looks its best

# CHECKLIST 3 — FERTILIZATION & TURF TREATMENT

> **Licensing gate — read first.** Commercial application of fertilizer and pesticides requires a **state applicator license in most states**, often with separate categories for turf and ornamentals, plus business licensing and record-keeping. Requirements, exam schedules, and re-certification **vary by state — verify with your state Department of Agriculture before applying anything for pay.** Applying without a license carries real penalties and will not be covered by your insurance.

- [ ] Confirm current applicator license and any required registration
- [ ] Identify grass type and confirm the product suits it
- [ ] Read the label completely — the label is the law
- [ ] Check weather: no application before heavy rain, wind, or extreme heat
- [ ] Confirm client notification requirements and posting/flagging rules for your state
- [ ] Calibrate the spreader or sprayer before the first application of the day
- [ ] Apply at labeled rate; avoid overlap burn
- [ ] Keep granular product off hard surfaces; sweep or blow back onto turf immediately
- [ ] Never allow product to reach storm drains or water features
- [ ] Post notification flags where required
- [ ] Advise client on watering-in, re-entry interval, and pet safety
- [ ] **Record the application** — date, product, EPA registration number, rate, area, applicator, weather. Record-keeping is legally required in most states and is your defense if a claim ever comes.
- [ ] Secure and store chemicals per label; keep Safety Data Sheets in the vehicle

# CHECKLIST 4 — AERATION & OVERSEEDING

*The highest-value add-on you can sell to an existing client, and the one that visibly improves a struggling lawn.*

- [ ] Confirm correct season for the grass type (fall for most cool-season; late spring/early summer for warm-season) — verify regionally
- [ ] Require the client to mark all irrigation heads, pet fence wire, and shallow lines — **non-negotiable for this service**
- [ ] Confirm soil moisture: moist, not saturated, not bone dry
- [ ] Mow slightly lower than usual before aerating
- [ ] Core aerate with multiple passes on compacted areas
- [ ] Leave cores on the surface to break down — explain this to the client in advance so it isn't a complaint
- [ ] Apply seed at the labeled rate for the species and purpose
- [ ] Apply starter fertilizer if appropriate and licensed
- [ ] Provide written watering instructions — seed dies without them, and the client will blame the seed
- [ ] Set expectations: germination timeline, mowing hold, traffic restrictions
- [ ] Schedule the follow-up visit

---

# CHECKLIST 5 — FALL CLEANUP & WINTERIZATION

*Where the season's revenue is either captured or left on the ground. Price it by the property, and expect multiple visits during heavy leaf drop.*

- [ ] Set scope with the client: single cleanup vs. multiple leaf visits
- [ ] Remove leaves from turf, beds, and hardscape
- [ ] Clear leaves from window wells, fence lines, drains, and gutters if in scope
- [ ] Mulch-mow light leaf cover where appropriate (returns nutrients and saves labor)
- [ ] Haul or dispose of debris per your agreement and local regulations
- [ ] Final cut at the appropriate height for winter
- [ ] Cut back perennials and ornamental grasses per plant type and client preference
- [ ] Apply late-season fertilizer if licensed and regionally appropriate
- [ ] Note snow-stake or irrigation-blowout needs; refer or quote
- [ ] Winterize your own equipment: fuel stabilizer, blades sharpened, batteries tended
- [ ] Before/after photos
- [ ] **Talk to every client about next season before you leave.** Signed spring agreements in November are the difference between a route and a scramble.

## Building the Season

Sell the **year**, not the visit. A client on a maintenance agreement with a spring cleanup, weekly mowing, an aeration in the right season, and a fall cleanup is worth several times a client who calls when the grass gets tall — and costs you a fraction of the effort to keep.

The route is the asset. Density is profit: ten lawns on one street beats twenty scattered across town, every single time.
