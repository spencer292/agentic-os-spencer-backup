## F1 — original analysis, retained for the record

**What.** Washington's 2026 exempt salary threshold is **2.25 × minimum wage = $1,541.70/week =
$80,168.40/year**, and it applies to employers of every size. An employee paid a salary below that
is **non-exempt and owed overtime**, regardless of job title, regardless of duties, and regardless
of having agreed to a salary.

Got Moles has at least one salaried employee — Cory Ventura, the Operator seat. His salary figure is
not recorded anywhere in this system.

**Why it matters.** If the salary is below $80,168.40 and he has worked over 40 hours in any week —
which for an Operator running field operations is close to certain — unpaid overtime is owed. WA
wage claims carry a three-year lookback, plus interest, and **double damages for a willful
violation**. This is the single most expensive item on this page, and it compounds every week it
stays unresolved.

It is also precisely what an HR retainer exists to catch. The threshold moved on 2026-01-01 and
again the year before. If Cascade HR did not raise it, that is the clearest available answer to
whether the retainer is earning its fee.

**Do next.**
1. Get Cory's current annual salary.
2. If it is at or above $80,168.40 — confirm the duties test also passes, record the conclusion, and
   close this finding.
3. If it is below — **stop and call an employment attorney before doing anything else.** Do not
   reclassify, do not backfill timesheets, do not discuss it with the employee first. Correcting a
   misclassification badly is worse than the misclassification. This is on the escalation list in
   `SKILL.md` for a reason.
4. Recheck every January when the threshold reindexes. It rises to 2.5× minimum wage by 2028, so
   anyone near the line will cross it without a pay cut.

Source: L&I, changes to Washington overtime rules. Verify by 2026-12-15.

## F2 — Accident Prevention Program may not exist · SEVERITY: HIGH · OPEN

**What.** Washington requires **every** employer, at any headcount, to maintain a written Accident
Prevention Program. It is among the first documents DOSH asks for in an inspection, and its absence
is a citation on its own before any actual hazard is considered.

No APP is present anywhere in this system.

**Why it matters.** The crew drives all day, handles traps, works alone on private property, and
deals with dogs. The exposure is real, not theoretical. A workplace injury with no APP and no
documented safety orientation is a much worse position than the same injury with both.

**Do next.** Ask Cascade HR whether they produced one and where it lives. If not, it gets built —
it is a short document and a genuine legal requirement, and its absence is a second data point on
what the retainer has been buying.

## F4 — Fueling before the first job starts the paid day · SEVERITY: MEDIUM · OPEN, DIAGNOSED

**Spencer, 2026-08-26:** "the only thing a technician might have to do before the first job is get
fuel."

That is a precise answer, and it identifies the exact issue rather than clearing it.

**Why fuel is not a neutral errand.** Fueling a company vehicle the tech is required to drive is
generally treated as **integral and indispensable** to the day's work — not a preliminary errand like
commuting. Once a compensable activity of the day begins, the **continuous workday** has started, and
travel after it is on the clock. Washington is more employee-favorable than the federal baseline
here: L&I treats employer-required travel outside the ordinary home-to-work commute as hours worked.

So on a day where a tech fuels on the way in, the likely reading is:

- Home → gas station: still an ordinary commute, unpaid
- **Fueling: paid**
- **Gas station → first stop: paid** — the workday has already begun

The current rule, "hours = job 1 to last job, commute unpaid", is correct on every day where nobody
fuels first. On fuel days it starts the clock slightly late.

**Magnitude.** Small per instance, and it multiplies. Fill in the real numbers:

`(fuel minutes + station-to-first-stop minutes) × fuel stops per week × number of techs × hourly rate`

At roughly 15 minutes, twice a week, across five techs, that is about 2.5 hours a week — call it
$3,000 a year, against a three-year lookback, with double damages available for a willful violation.
Not existential. Also completely avoidable.

**The fix costs nothing.** Do not litigate whether fuel time is compensable — **move the fuel stop
inside the workday.** A written rule that techs fuel *between stops* during the day, never before the
first job, converts an ambiguous unpaid activity into time that is already being paid. No extra cost,
no back-pay question, no argument to have later. It also loses nothing operationally: the tank does
not care when it gets filled.

Second-best, if fueling first is genuinely unavoidable on some days: the tech clocks in at the fuel
stop on those days, and the day runs from there.

**Do next.**
1. Confirm how often this actually happens — occasional or routine changes the size, not the answer.
2. Issue the written rule: fuel between stops, never before the first job. Add it to the tech
   handbook and to training Module 1 (the day and the route).
3. Once the rule is in place and being followed, this finding closes and the existing
   "job 1 to last job" hours rule becomes accurate again with no exception attached.
